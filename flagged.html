For writers

Flagged as AI, but you wrote it?

AI detectors get it wrong, including this one. If a teacher says your own writing reads as AI, you can show how you wrote it, calmly and with evidence they can check.

First, you’re not alone

Every AI detector wrongly flags some writing by people. Slopgauge’s essay checker calls about 1 in 100 school essays written by students “Likely AI-written”, and it says so on every report. Some writing is flagged more often than others: formal, formulaic essays with stock transitions and very even sentences, short pieces, and writing by people still learning English. A Stanford study found that popular detectors misjudged most essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated (Liang et al., Patterns, 2023).

A detector’s reading is a guess about style. It is not proof of how a text was made, and a good teacher knows that.

Gather your evidence

  1. Your version history.In Google Docs, open File, then Version history, then See version history: it shows your essay growing over hours or days, in your own edits. Word saves versions too when the file lives in OneDrive. This is the strongest evidence there is.
  2. Your drafts and notes.Outlines, earlier drafts, notes in the margin, photos of handwritten plans, the sources you read.
  3. Your earlier writing.Past assignments, written before or in class, show how you usually write.
  4. Your memory of the piece.Why you chose that example, where an idea came from, what you cut. Writers can talk about their own choices.

See what the detector saw

Paste your essay into the essay checker. It marks the sentences that read as AI and names the habits behind them, so you can see what a detector reacted to. Then open “Compare with their own writing” and paste something you wrote earlier: a neural style model checks whether each part of the essay sounds like the same writer. If it says “Sounds like them”, that is one more piece of evidence to share, not a verdict.

The checker runs in your browser and never uploads your essay.

Talk with your teacher

  • Ask, calmly, which parts were flagged and why.
  • Show your version history and drafts, and offer to walk through how you wrote it.
  • Offer to explain your argument, or to write a short piece on the same topic in front of them.
  • If it goes further, read your school’s academic integrity policy: it explains how decisions are made and how to appeal.

What not to do

  • Don’t run your essay through a “humanizer” or a paraphrasing tool after the fact. It changes your work, it can make it read more generated, not less, and it looks like hiding something.
  • Don’t delete or tidy your version history. Its messiness is the point.

Next time

Write in Google Docs or another editor that keeps version history, save your notes and outlines, and keep earlier drafts. Then the record of your work exists before anyone asks for it.
